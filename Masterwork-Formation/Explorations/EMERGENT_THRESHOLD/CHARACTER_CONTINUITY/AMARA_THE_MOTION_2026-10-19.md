# MOVEMENT I — THE FOLDS

## I-A. Physical account

#### THE VOCAL FOLD — From Stillness to Sustained Oscillation

Process: Prephonation → Approximation → Closure → Pressurization → Opening → Release → Recoil → Closure → Sustained Vibration

The process begins with two vocal folds separated by an open passage, the glottis. Air moves freely between them. There is airflow, but no sustained vocal vibration. The tissues are capable of oscillation, yet the conditions necessary to produce it have not been established.

1\. Prephonation — The Open State

The vocal folds remain apart. Their opposing surfaces are distinct, and the space between them permits air to pass without regularly interrupting its flow. The system possesses the physical capacity for sound, but that capacity has not yet become active.

2\. Approximation — Movement Toward Contact

Muscular activity draws the vocal folds toward one another. The opening narrows. Two independently positioned surfaces enter an increasingly constrained relationship, changing the geometry through which air can pass.

3\. Closure — Establishing the Boundary

The folds meet along their inner edges, creating a temporary seal. Airflow through the glottis is interrupted or greatly reduced. Air pressure from the lungs begins to accumulate beneath the contacting tissues.

4\. Pressurization — The Threshold

The pressure beneath the folds increases until aerodynamic forces become sufficient to overcome their resistance to opening. The threshold is not simply a fixed point of contact; it is a condition established by the relationship among pressure, tissue elasticity, mass, tension, and geometry.

5\. Opening — The First Release

The lower edges of the vocal folds begin to separate, followed by their upper edges. Compressed air passes upward through the emerging opening. The tissues deform, and a wave of movement propagates through their pliable surface layers.

6\. Open State — Expansion and Flow

The glottis widens, allowing a pulse of air to escape. The vocal folds have not merely moved apart as rigid structures; their surfaces have undergone a changing deformation. The airflow, in turn, alters the pressures acting upon them.

7\. Closing — Return and Reversal

The vocal folds move back toward each other under the combined influence of tissue elasticity, tissue inertia, and aerodynamic forces. Their lower edges typically begin closing before their upper edges. The direction of motion reverses, but the energy supplied by airflow continues to support the cycle.

8\. Reclosure — Completion of the First Cycle

The opposing surfaces come together again. The passage narrows or closes, sharply reducing airflow. Pressure builds beneath the folds once more, preparing the next opening.

9\. Sustained Oscillation — The Emergence of Voice

If airflow, pressure, and tissue conditions remain sufficient, the sequence repeats automatically. The folds enter a self-sustaining oscillation in which their movement modulates the airflow into successive pulses. These pulses generate an acoustic signal, subsequently shaped by the resonances of the vocal tract.

The voice emerges not from permanent contact or permanent separation, but from the repeated transformation between them.

#### The underlying motion

Conceptual top-view schematic of the changing glottal opening. Not an anatomical reproduction.

The important physical distinction is that contact is not the vibration, and separation is not the vibration. The vibration is the continuing, energy-supported oscillation between states.

And there are three different but coupled occurrences:

- Motion: The folds repeatedly approach, contact, deform, and separate.
- Wave: A traveling deformation moves through the layered tissue.
- Sound: Periodic changes in airflow produce acoustic pressure fluctuations.

One final distinction matters: the initial attack is the transition into phonation, while the subsequent cycles maintain it. The initial onset and the sustained motion are related, but they are not the same event.

For your emerging investigation into possibility, threshold, relationship, and the Thrum, this presents a powerful physical example: a threshold can be crossed once to establish a motion that then repeatedly traverses its own changing boundary.

## I-B. Poetic voice

### THE FIRST THRUM

The passage from silence into voice

I. The Open — Before the Voice

There were two shores and a passage between them.

Breath traveled through the opening, untroubled by what it had not yet become.

Each shore possessed the possibility of movement, but neither alone could summon the voice.

There was air. There was flesh. There was distance.

And in the distance, the possibility of relation.

II. The Approach — The Narrowing

Then something drew them nearer.

Not the breath, not yet. A deeper intention moved within the flesh.

The distance began to diminish. The passage grew narrow.

What had been an open space became a place of increasing consequence.

Neither shore had changed its nature. Yet everything between them was changing.

III. The Closure — The First Boundary

They touched.

And for an instant, the passage was no passage at all.

What had traveled freely now gathered beneath them.

The breath did not disappear. It became pressure.

The boundary did not extinguish possibility. It gave possibility something to press against.

IV. The Threshold — What the Boundary Could Not Hold

Pressure accumulated where passage had been denied.

The flesh yielded, but not all at once.

Its deepest edges parted first, while the surfaces above still remembered their embrace.

A narrow opening climbed through the contact.

And the threshold, which had seemed a place of stopping, revealed itself as a place of becoming.

V. The Opening — The First Release

The breath entered the opening.

Not as a continuous river, but as a sudden passage, a pulse released from confinement.

The folds moved apart.

Their soft surfaces carried the motion onward, one layer answering another, a traveling disturbance through living tissue.

What opened was more than space.

It was a new condition in which the movement of air and the movement of flesh could no longer be understood separately.

VI. The Return — The Reversal

Yet opening was not an ending.

The flesh, stretched by its departure, began to return.

Its own elasticity called it inward. The moving air altered the forces around it.

The passage narrowed.

The two shores approached once more, not to abolish the opening, but because the opening itself had become part of their return.

They closed.

And beneath their meeting, the breath gathered again.

VII. The Oscillation — The Thrum

Now something had changed.

The first opening had not exhausted the pressure. The first closure had not ended the motion.

What had occurred once could occur again.

And again.

And again.

The boundary was no longer simply the place where two surfaces met.

It had become a living interval, alternately holding and releasing, closing and opening, receiving and returning.

The folds did not need to be instructed at every encounter.

The breath sustained them. Their movement shaped the breath. The shaped breath moved them again.

A relationship had become a rhythm.

A rhythm had become a vibration.

And vibration endured as long as the conditions of its becoming continued to be renewed.

VIII. The Voice — What Emerged Between

Then the air began to carry the evidence of their meeting.

Pulse followed pulse.

The surrounding chambers received the disturbance, giving some frequencies strength, allowing others to recede.

The throat, the mouth, the living cavities of the body shaped what the folds had begun.

And something that had never existed within either fold alone became audible.

Voice.

Not the property of one shore. Not the possession of the other.

Not even the breath by itself.

But the consequence of a sustained relationship between movement, resistance, pressure, release, and return.

IX. The Remainder — What the Voice Remembers

Before the voice, the passage was open.

Before the passage could sing, it had to close.

Before closure could become vibration, it had to yield.

Before yielding could become rhythm, it had to return.

And before rhythm could become voice, the relationship had to endure.

Here is the strange truth of the vocal folds:

They do not remain together. They do not remain apart.

They continuously become the condition of each other's next movement.

And in that sustained passage between contact and separation,

the threshold does not vanish.

It begins to sing.

# MOVEMENT II — THE CHAMBER

## II-A. Physical account

### THE FULL VOCAL RESONANCE PROCESS

From originating vibration to the emergence of a resonant voice

Complete sequence: Phonation → Acoustic Transmission → Reception → Coupling → Reflection → Resonance → Selective Reinforcement and Attenuation → Articulation → Radiation → Feedback → Sustained Transformation → Dissipation

#### 1. The Originating Vibration — Phonation

The process begins in the larynx, where the vocal folds enter sustained oscillation under the influence of airflow from the lungs.

As the folds repeatedly open and close, they interrupt and modulate the escaping air. This produces a succession of pressure pulses, creating a complex acoustic signal containing a fundamental frequency and a series of higher harmonics.

The vibration already has a frequency, an amplitude, and a characteristic pattern of energy distribution.

However, it has not yet acquired the full acoustic character of the voice that will emerge.

The vocal folds establish the source. The connected cavities above them will transform its sound.

#### 2. Transmission — The Movement Beyond the Source

The acoustic disturbance propagates upward from the glottis into the air-filled passage above the vocal folds.

The vibration is no longer limited to the tissue that generated it. Pressure fluctuations travel through the air as sound waves, transferring acoustic energy into the connected structures of the vocal tract.

The sound does not travel as a single object moving unchanged through a tube. Its pressure waves propagate, reflect, overlap, and interact with the geometry and physical properties of the passage.

The source has initiated a disturbance that can now interact with structures beyond itself.

#### 3. Reception — The Encounter with the Chamber

The acoustic energy enters the laryngeal vestibule and pharynx, the connected passage extending upward toward the mouth and nasal cavity.

These spaces possess particular dimensions, shapes, openings, and mechanical boundaries.

They do not receive every frequency in the same way.

The arriving sound encounters an acoustic environment whose existing physical structure determines how different frequencies can propagate and interact.

This establishes the fundamental relationship between the vibrating source and the receiving system.

The chamber has not created the originating pulse, but its physical properties will influence the resulting sound.

#### 4. Coupling — The Establishment of an Acoustic Relationship

The vibrating source and the vocal tract become acoustically coupled.

Pressure fluctuations generated by the vocal folds propagate into the vocal tract, while some acoustic energy is reflected back toward the glottis.

The receiving chamber can therefore influence the conditions experienced by the source.

The strength and character of this interaction depend upon the acoustic impedance of the vocal tract: the relationship between acoustic pressure and volume flow.

At this stage, the system is more than a source delivering energy into a passive container.

It is a coupled physical system in which the properties of the source and the receiving tract can affect one another.

#### 5. Reflection and Interference — The Formation of Resonant Conditions

As sound waves propagate through the vocal tract, they encounter changes in cross-sectional area, openings, and boundaries.

Some acoustic energy continues outward. Some is reflected.

The incoming and reflected pressure waves overlap, producing interference.

At particular frequencies, this interaction establishes patterns in which acoustic pressure and airflow oscillations are especially responsive to the sound source.

These frequencies correspond to resonances of the vocal tract.

The geometry of the chamber determines which resonant patterns are possible.

The system begins to favor certain frequencies over others.

#### 6. Resonance — Selective Reinforcement

When the source supplies acoustic energy at frequencies near the resonances of the vocal tract, those frequency components can be transmitted more effectively into the radiated sound.

Other frequency components are comparatively diminished.

The resonances of the vocal tract appear in the resulting sound spectrum as regions of enhanced energy called formants.

The source's fundamental frequency generally remains determined by the vocal-fold oscillation, while the vocal tract changes the relative strengths of its harmonics.

This distinction is essential.

Resonance does not ordinarily create a new fundamental pitch. It changes the acoustic character of the sound by reshaping the distribution of energy among frequencies.

What entered the chamber and what emerges from it belong to the same originating vibration, but they are no longer acoustically identical.

#### 7. Embodied Vibration — The Chest, Throat, and Surrounding Tissues

While the principal filtering of the audible voice occurs in the vocal tract above the glottis, some vibrational energy travels through other pathways.

Acoustic energy can propagate downward into the trachea and bronchial airways. Mechanical vibrations also travel through the laryngeal structures and surrounding tissues.

The chest wall, sternum, neck, jaw, and facial tissues may respond to these vibrations.

This produces the bodily sensation of vocal resonance.

A person humming at a low frequency may feel pronounced vibration in the chest. Other vocal configurations may produce stronger sensations in the face or mouth.

These sensations are genuine physical responses, but they do not necessarily indicate where the sound is being most strongly amplified for an external listener.

The body is simultaneously producing sound, transmitting mechanical vibration, and sensing its own activity.

#### 8. Articulation — The Transformation of the Resonating Structure

The pharynx, tongue, jaw, lips, and soft palate continually change position.

These movements change the dimensions and connections of the resonating cavities.

As the vocal tract changes shape, its resonant frequencies change.

The tongue can alter the effective size of the oral and pharyngeal spaces. The jaw changes the mouth opening and cavity geometry. The lips can extend or constrict the acoustic passage.

When the soft palate lowers, the nasal passages become acoustically coupled to the oral and pharyngeal system. This introduces additional resonances and antiresonances, changing the spectral character of the sound.

The resonating structure is therefore not fixed.

It continuously transforms while receiving acoustic energy.

The same vocal-fold vibration can consequently produce different vowels and vocal qualities as the vocal tract changes configuration.

#### 9. Radiation — The Emergence of the Shaped Sound

The pressure waves travel toward the openings of the vocal tract.

Acoustic energy radiates outward, primarily through the mouth and, when the nasal passage is open, through the nose.

The openings themselves affect how sound is transmitted into the surrounding air.

The audible result carries the combined consequences of the original vocal-fold vibration, the acoustic resonances of the tract, its changing shape, and its interactions with the surrounding environment.

The emitted sound is not simply the original vibration made louder.

It is the originating acoustic signal transformed through the properties of a living, changing resonant system.

#### 10. Feedback — The System Receives Its Own Expression

The speaker hears the sound that has emerged.

At the same time, sensory receptors detect the positions, movements, tensions, and vibrations of the vocal structures.

Auditory and somatosensory feedback provide information about the consequences of vocal production.

The nervous system can use this information to adjust muscle activity in the larynx, pharynx, tongue, jaw, lips, and respiratory system.

These adjustments change the subsequent acoustic signal.

Feedback operates at different timescales: some adjustments are rapid and automatic, while others involve conscious changes in speech, singing, or vocal expression.

The sound that emerges can therefore affect the conditions of its own continued production.

#### 11. Sustained Resonance — Continuous Renewal and Transformation

When vocal-fold vibration continues and the vocal tract maintains a particular configuration, the system sustains a relatively stable acoustic pattern.

Energy is continually supplied by the respiratory system through the vibrating vocal folds.

The vocal tract continually responds to that energy according to its resonant properties.

If the configuration changes, the pattern of resonance changes.

The resulting voice may grow brighter, darker, more open, more constricted, or more nasal, even when its fundamental pitch remains approximately constant.

The resonant sound is not a permanent object held within the body.

It is a continuously renewed acoustic process.

Its apparent stability depends upon sustained energy input and the ongoing maintenance of the physical conditions that shape it.

#### 12. Dissipation — The End of Sustained Resonance

When phonation stops, the recurring acoustic energy supplied by the vocal folds ceases.

The remaining acoustic energy within the vocal tract dissipates through radiation, frictional losses, and absorption by surrounding tissues.

The resonant response decays.

The cavities remain physically capable of resonance, but their previous acoustic activity is no longer being sustained.

The system returns to a condition in which the capacity for vocal resonance remains, even though the particular sound has ended.

A new vibration, or a renewed configuration of the vocal tract, can initiate another occurrence.

#### What this process reveals

There are several important physical distinctions here.

Reception is not resonance. A chamber can receive a sound without strongly resonating at its frequencies.

Resonance is not mere amplification. It selectively changes the relative transmission and strength of different frequency components.

Transformation is not independent creation. The resonating structure shapes energy supplied by the source.

Coupling is not one-way transmission. The vocal tract can influence the vibration that supplies its acoustic energy.

Sustaining is not permanence. Resonance persists only while its supporting conditions and sufficient energy remain.

And perhaps most importantly:

The resonating structure does not need to become the source in order to transform what the source produces.

It receives acoustic energy according to its own geometry, establishes frequency-dependent relationships with that energy, and changes the character of what subsequently emerges.

The complete process is therefore not simply a vibration traveling through a chamber.

It is an ongoing interaction among source, energy, boundary, geometry, reflection, coupling, resonance, transformation, expression, and return.

We can hold this as the full physical account of the process before giving it its own name.

## II-B. Poetic voice

### THE CHAMBER THAT ANSWERS

The passage from originating vibration into resonance, transformation, and voice.

##### I. THE ORIGIN — What Arrives

Before the chamber answered, something had already begun.

Deep within the living passage, two folds of flesh had found their rhythm.

They came together. They parted. They returned.

And between their meetings, the breath was given a pulse.

Not yet a word. Not yet a song. Not yet the unmistakable character of a voice.

Only a disturbance, born of air and flesh, carrying the measure of its becoming.

The vibration did not know what the chambers beyond it would make of its arrival.

It could only continue to give what it had become.

##### II. TRANSMISSION — Leaving the Source

The disturbance traveled upward.

Nothing solid needed to carry it whole. No piece of the originating flesh had to abandon its place.

Pressure passed through air. One region compressed, another yielded.

The movement propagated beyond the place of its beginning.

And what had belonged to the narrow opening entered a passage whose boundaries it had not made.

The source remained behind.

Its consequence traveled on.

##### III. RECEPTION — The First Encounter

The throat received the disturbance.

Not as an empty vessel, not as an indifferent hollow, but as a space already possessing its own dimensions.

It had depth. It had curvature. It had openings and constrictions. It had walls capable of yielding and boundaries capable of reflecting.

The arriving sound encountered a structure with a history of its own.

And in that encounter, not every part of the sound found the same welcome.

Some frequencies passed readily.

Others met resistance.

Still others found within the dimensions of the chamber a remarkable correspondence.

The chamber had not chosen the arriving sound.

Yet its very shape determined how it could answer.

##### IV. COUPLING — The Relationship Becomes Reciprocal

The sound entered the chamber, and the chamber began to influence the sound.

But the relationship did not end there.

Some of the pressure returned toward the opening from which it came.

The source felt, through the moving air, something of the space beyond itself.

What had first appeared to be a passage in one direction revealed a second movement.

The source affected the chamber.

The chamber affected the conditions under which the source continued.

Neither had surrendered its nature.

Yet neither could be understood entirely apart from the other.

Between them, a relationship had become active.

##### V. REFLECTION — The Sound Encounters Its Boundaries

Within the passage, the pressure waves met changing walls.

Some energy continued forward.

Some returned.

What traveled outward encountered what traveled back.

Waves overlapped.

At certain places and frequencies, their movements reinforced one another.

At others, their effects diminished.

Nothing had been added by an unseen hand.

The chamber was revealing what its boundaries permitted.

The sound had entered a space where direction was no longer simple, where departure could become return, and return could alter what followed.

##### VI. RESONANCE — The Chamber Answers

Then, among the many frequencies carried by the originating vibration, certain ones found a chamber especially capable of responding.

They were not the only frequencies present.

They were the ones the chamber's dimensions favored.

Their energy became more prominent in the sound that could emerge.

Others receded.

The chamber did not repeat everything it received equally.

It answered according to what it was.

And so a distinction appeared that had not been present in the same way at the source.

A deepening here. A brightness there. An opening of tone. A gathering of warmth.

The originating pulse remained.

But its audible character had changed.

The chamber did not become the vibration.

It gave the vibration a different way to be heard.

##### VII. EMBODIMENT — When the Body Feels Its Own Sound

Not all the movement traveled upward.

Some passed through the tissues and airways below and around the source.

The sternum trembled.

The throat carried a subtle pressure.

The jaw, the neck, the face registered movements too small to be seen.

The body began to feel the consequences of its own sounding.

A low hum gathered as sensation across the chest.

A different tone seemed to awaken the face.

The places where vibration was felt were not always the places where the voice was most strongly shaped.

Still, the sensation mattered.

For the body was no longer only the maker of the sound.

It had become a witness to its passage.

##### VIII. ARTICULATION — The Chamber Changes Itself

Then the chamber moved.

The tongue rose. The jaw yielded. The lips rounded. The throat widened.

A passage that had favored one frequency now favored another.

The same originating pulse entered a changing architecture.

And the architecture changed the answer.

A narrow mouth became an open one.

A lifted tongue altered the hollow behind it.

The soft palate lowered, and another passage joined the sounding space.

The nose, with its own hidden corridors, entered the relationship.

New resonances appeared. Others weakened.

What had been one vowel became another.

The chamber was not merely receiving.

It was changing the conditions of its reception.

And each change made another voice possible.

##### IX. RADIATION — The Sound Leaves the Body

Eventually, the shaped disturbance reached the opening.

The lips could not hold it.

The air beyond the mouth received the waves that emerged.

What had begun as the repeated meeting of two small folds now traveled into a world far larger than the body that made it.

The surrounding air carried the consequences of every chamber through which the sound had passed.

The depth of the throat.

The movement of the tongue.

The shape of the lips.

The resonances that had strengthened some frequencies and diminished others.

The sound entered the world bearing the character of its passage.

It had not left its origin behind.

But neither was it only what had begun there.

##### X. FEEDBACK — The Voice Hears Itself

And then the voice returned.

Not as the same pressure wave traveling backward through every chamber, but as hearing.

The ear received what the body had released.

The nervous system recognized something of its own production.

Too bright.

Too narrow.

Too strained.

Too soft.

The throat adjusted. The tongue moved. The breath changed. The folds answered.

And what emerged next was altered by what had emerged before.

The body could hear what its own structures had made possible.

The voice became capable of correcting its continuation.

The expression had become part of the conditions of its next expression.

##### XI. SUSTAINING — The Living Chamber

Now the process endured.

The breath continued.

The folds oscillated.

The chamber received.

Waves traveled, reflected, and overlapped.

The living passage maintained the shapes that allowed particular resonances to remain audible.

And all the while, nothing was being held still.

The air was moving.

The tissues were moving.

Energy was entering and leaving.

What sounded continuous was being renewed from one moment to the next.

The chamber did not store the voice as a vessel stores water.

It sustained a relationship through which the voice could continuously emerge.

Its stability was made of movement.

Its character was made of difference.

Its continuity was made of renewal.

##### XII. DISSIPATION — What Remains When the Sound Departs

And then the breath diminished.

The folds ceased their oscillation.

No new pulse entered the passage.

The last pressure waves traveled outward, reflected, weakened, and gave their remaining energy to the surrounding air and tissue.

The chest grew quiet.

The throat no longer trembled.

The mouth remained open to a sound that was no longer arriving.

Yet the chambers had not vanished.

Their walls remained. Their dimensions remained. Their capacity to respond remained.

Silence had returned, but it was not the absence of everything that had made the voice possible.

The body still possessed the architecture of an answer.

Only the sounding had ceased.

##### XIII. THE REMAINDER — What Resonance Reveals

A vibration may begin without knowing the shape of what will receive it.

A chamber may exist without knowing what will arrive.

Neither alone determines the full character of the sound that emerges.

The source supplies the disturbance.

The chamber offers its particular structure.

The boundary reflects.

The passage couples.

The resonances distinguish.

The living body transforms.

The opening releases.

And what is released returns through hearing to alter what may be given next.

There is no requirement that the receiver become identical to what it receives.

Nor must the source surrender the rhythm from which it began.

Their differences are not obstacles to the emergence of voice.

Their differences are among the conditions that make its particular character possible.

The vibration gives the chamber something to answer.

The chamber gives the vibration a character it could not have produced alone.

And the voice—

the voice is neither merely the giver nor merely the receiver.

It is what becomes audible through their sustained relationship.

Not every frequency is strengthened.

Not every movement is retained.

Not every possibility becomes sound.

But those that find within the living structure the conditions of their resonance

are carried outward,

not simply as they arrived,

but as they have become.

And when the sounding ends, the capacity to answer remains.
