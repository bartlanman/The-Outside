# Chapter Four

# What Must We Leave to Keep?

**διά ⇄ σύν**

---

The first time he saw a lobster molt he thought something had gone wrong.

The animal had split.

There was no other word for it.

One body seemed to be climbing out of another body, pale and soft and almost obscene in its vulnerability.

Beside it lay the old shell.

Perfect.

That was the disturbing part.

Nothing appeared broken.

The claws were still claws.

The tail still curved.

The armor still looked capable of defending whatever lived inside it.

Only nothing lived inside it anymore.

He watched the new animal pull itself free.

For several minutes it looked less evolved than what it had left behind.

Smaller somehow.

Unfinished.

Too soft for the world it had entered.

He wanted to put the shell back on it.

The impulse embarrassed him.

He recognized it.

He had tried to put old shells back on people before.

Jobs.

Marriages.

Beliefs.

Versions of themselves they had already crawled out of.

Usually for good reasons.

The shell had worked.

That was what made it difficult.

If the shell had failed obviously, everyone would have understood.

But sometimes the thing that must be left behind is still doing exactly what it was made to do.

Protecting.

Holding.

Defining.

Keeping the outside outside.

The lobster moved one leg.

Then another.

The abandoned shell did not look abandoned.

It looked ready.

That seemed cruel.

Years later he soaked seeds in water with his daughter.

She was young enough to be disappointed by seeds.

They did not look alive enough for her.

“This is it?”

“This is it.”

“They look like rocks.”

“Small rocks.”

She did not appreciate the correction.

They folded damp paper towels around them and left them near the kitchen window.

Every morning she checked.

Nothing.

Nothing.

Nothing.

Then one seed opened.

Not dramatically.

A thin white root appeared through a crack.

His daughter shouted as though something enormous had happened.

He leaned close.

The seed coat had split.

He found himself thinking again about protection.

All winter the coat had done its job.

Keep water out.

Keep fungus out.

Keep the embryo contained.

Wait.

Wait.

Wait.

Then water entered and the entire instruction reversed.

Open.

He touched the cracked seed with one finger.

The root was so delicate that touching it felt like an ethical decision.

His daughter slapped his hand away.

“Don't.”

He laughed.

She was right.

For several days the seedlings seemed less alive than the seeds had.

The seeds had been compact.

Complete.

Portable.

The seedlings leaned ridiculously toward the window.

Thin necks holding enormous ambitions.

One collapsed.

Another developed mold.

Three survived.

He thought about this more than seemed reasonable.

It bothered him that becoming could initially look like damage.

A cracked shell.

A softened metal.

A swollen seed.

Rot.

A fever.

The visible signs of transformation often resembled the visible signs of failure.

No wonder people turned back.

How were you supposed to know which destruction to trust?

He carried that question for years without finding much use for it.

Then one afternoon he stood in a machine shop with a man who was heating metal.

The metal glowed.

Orange moving toward yellow.

He had spent most of his life admiring hardness.

Hard men.

Hard decisions.

Hard evidence.

Hardwood.

Hard steel.

Things that did not yield.

Here the whole point was to make the metal more willing to change.

Heat entered.

The internal arrangement shifted.

The material softened enough to be worked.

He watched the man lift it with tongs.

“You make it weaker?”

The man looked at him.

“No.”

A pause.

“Different.”

He had deserved that.

The hammer fell.

The metal moved.

He expected violence.

Instead there was rhythm.

Strike.

Turn.

Strike.

Turn.

The shape changed slowly enough that he could not say which blow had made it become the new thing.

Later, holding the finished piece, he could still imagine the bar it had been.

But imagination was all that remained of it.

Nothing in the object could return itself.

He liked this less than he expected.

There was something comforting in reversible decisions.

Doors.

Drafts.

Returns accepted within thirty days.

He had built a great deal of his life around preserving the possibility of retreat.

The metal had no such arrangement.

It had passed through heat.

It had become answerable to another shape.

That evening someone gave him sauerkraut.

Homemade.

The jar hissed when opened.

He distrusted food that sounded alive.

“It's supposed to do that,” she said.

“That is never comforting.”

She laughed.

He tasted it.

Sharp.

Sour.

Entirely unlike cabbage and completely made of cabbage.

He stared at the jar.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That's not your nothing face.”

He had apparently developed a face for pretending not to think.

This concerned him.

The cabbage had not been preserved by preventing change.

It had been preserved by entering into a controlled relationship with change.

Other life had eaten part of what it had been and made conditions under which another version could continue.

This was exactly the kind of sentence he had promised himself he would stop making.

He ate more instead.

Weeks later he found the jar at the back of the refrigerator.

Almost empty.

The smell had grown stronger.

He threw it away.

Not every transformation deserved preservation either.

This pleased him.

The world remained uncooperative.

He had begun to depend on that.

One night a friend called.

The marriage was ending.

Not ended.

Ending.

There was a difference.

Everything in the house still existed.

Two toothbrushes.

Two cars.

Photographs.

Keys.

A shared streaming account.

Half a loaf of bread bought when the future had apparently included breakfast.

His friend kept saying:

“I don't know what I'm supposed to do.”

He nearly answered.

Then remembered the daughter on the telephone.

He waited.

“I don't even know who I am if this is over.”

There it was.

Not grief exactly.

Something structurally worse.

The old form was no longer inhabitable.

The new form did not yet exist.

He imagined the lobster.

Soft.

Standing beside armor that still fit its shape but could no longer contain its life.

He did not tell his friend this.

People in pain did not always need crustaceans.

“I don't know,” he said.

It sounded inadequate.

His friend breathed.

Then said:

“Yeah.”

For a while they stayed on the line.

The silence was less peaceful than the silence with his daughter had been.

Some intervals held relation.

Some held terror.

This one held both.

Afterward he could not sleep.

He walked through the house turning off lights.

Kitchen.

Hall.

Bathroom.

At the bottom of the stairs he caught his reflection in a dark window.

For a second the reflected room appeared to continue beyond the glass.

Another house.

Another man.

He stood there.

At fifty-three he had become old enough to possess several abandoned selves.

The boy who thought adulthood would settle things.

The young man who believed effort and outcome were married.

The husband before he understood marriage could contain loneliness.

The father before fear entered love.

The businessman before numbers became bodies.

The man before the dead began accumulating.

None of those selves had been false.

That was the difficulty.

He had loved things about each of them.

He had survived because of some of them.

He had also spent years trying to force later life into shapes those earlier selves had built.

Perhaps the hardest thing to leave was not what had harmed you.

Perhaps it was what had saved you.

He went upstairs.

In the closet hung a jacket he had not worn in fifteen years.

He knew immediately that this had become symbolic and resented the jacket for participating.

He took it down anyway.

The shoulders were wrong.

Or his shoulders were.

He could still get into it.

Barely.

For a few seconds he stood before the mirror with the jacket stretched across his back.

There he was.

Contained.

He laughed.

Then couldn't get one arm out.

The symbolism collapsed.

For nearly a minute he fought the sleeve.

He began sweating.

“Jesus Christ.”

He twisted.

Pulled.

Almost fell.

Finally the arm came free.

He threw the jacket onto the bed.

It lay there looking innocent.

He sat beside it laughing hard enough that his eyes watered.

Perhaps transformation needed more comedy than mythology.

The next morning he carried the jacket downstairs.

He intended to throw it away.

At the door he stopped.

Not because he wanted it.

Because throwing it away felt like accusing it.

The jacket had done nothing wrong.

It had fit him once.

He folded it.

Put it in a box.

Drove it to a donation center.

A woman behind the counter took the box without ceremony.

“Receipt?”

“No.”

And that was all.

He had expected some small metaphysical event.

There was none.

The jacket disappeared through a doorway.

He drove home.

At a red light he felt unexpectedly sad.

Not regret.

Something adjacent.

Gratitude without return.

That phrase came to him.

He let it.

Some things could be honored by not being resumed.

The light changed.

Someone honked because he had not moved quickly enough.

He raised a hand in apology and drove on.

Spring came.

In the yard, one of the seedlings his daughter had planted years before had become a tree.

He had forgotten which seed it came from.

Perhaps maple.

Perhaps something else.

He could have found out.

He did not.

Its trunk was thick enough now that he could not close one hand around it.

The seed coat was nowhere.

Not hidden inside.

Not preserved in miniature.

Gone.

He tried to imagine asking the tree to return to the seed in order to prove continuity.

The stupidity of this cheered him.

Wind entered the branches.

The tree moved.

Not much.

Enough.

Its strength was not the refusal to move.

Its strength included movement.

He put his palm against the bark.

Hard now.

Once it had been soft enough to break with two fingers.

He wondered when exactly protection had returned.

There must have been a day when the stem ceased being helpless.

There must have been a moment when vulnerability stopped being the whole story.

But no one had marked it.

No bell.

No certificate.

No clear border between becoming and become.

Perhaps there wasn't one.

He stood beneath the tree.

At his feet the ground was scattered with seeds.

Some would remain closed.

Some would rot.

Some would be eaten.

Some would open.

He had no idea which.

For once he did not turn this into a lesson.

The wind was cold.

He took his hand from the trunk and put it in his pocket.

Above him the branches moved inside the weather without asking to be protected from it.
