# Chapter One

# What Remains by Changing?

**διά ⇄ σύν**

---

There were things a man could change simply by touching them.

A room.  
A sentence.  
The course of an afternoon.

There were other things that changed because he had touched them, though he had not meant to change them at all.

And there were things that would not change for him.

This last category had taken him the longest to understand.

He used to think resistance was a kind of failure in the world.

A locked door meant the wrong key.  
A silence meant the wrong question.  
A memory that would not soften meant he had not yet found the correct way to remember it.

He had been raised, as most men are, among tools.

Hammer.  
Lever.  
Language.

Things for making the world yield.

Then one morning he read about a temple that had survived for centuries because they kept tearing it down.

Not ruin.

Not neglect.

Men arrived with cedar and measurements handed through generations and made again what their fathers had made, and what the fathers of their fathers had made, and when the new thing stood where the old thing had stood, they carried what mattered across.

He stared at this for a long time.

The building was gone.

The temple remained.

Outside his window the first leaves had begun turning at their edges.

He thought:

Maybe we have mistaken the body for the thing that survives.

But there was another man who made artworks whose completion was their disappearance.

Acid ate the fabric.

Time entered the work.

The thing did not fail because it vanished.

Vanishing was what it had been instructed to become.

So now there were two destructions before him.

One destroyed the object so the form could continue.

One destroyed the object because destruction was the form.

He could feel the distinction without being able to name it.

That was when he had learned to stop naming things too soon.

Names were useful.

They were also little fences.

Wind moved through the trees.

A branch knocked once against the gutter.

Somewhere far from him, monks—or priests, or students, he did not know enough yet to call them correctly—were carrying syllables across centuries.

Breath into ear.

Ear into breath.

A human chain devoted not to invention but to fidelity.

He had thought skill was what allowed a person to vary.

Here were people becoming more skilled so that something might vary less.

The thought disturbed him.

He liked things that opened.

Possibility.

Improvisation.

The phrase no one had used before.

But perhaps an opening required an edge.

Perhaps a river became a river because some ground refused it.

He wrote this down and crossed it out.

Too neat.

He was beginning to recognize neatness as one of his more dangerous appetites.

Later he found the archive.

A record could be wrong and still have to remain exactly wrong.

This offended something in him.

If Tuesday had been Wednesday, why preserve Tuesday?

Because Tuesday was what the witness wrote.

Because the mistake had happened too.

Because the past did not owe the future a cleaned copy of itself.

He sat back.

On his desk was a photograph of someone much younger than the person now carrying the same name.

The face in the photograph knew nothing of what would happen next.

He had always looked at old photographs with the arrogance of the survivor.

Poor fool, he would think.

You don't know yet.

But the boy in the photograph had one advantage over him.

He had actually been there.

The man had only memory.

And memory, he was beginning to understand, was a generous counterfeiter.

Across town someone was replacing a cracked pane of glass.

In another country someone was restoring a cathedral stone by stone.

Somewhere a conservator was making certain the new work could still be distinguished from the old.

Not because the repair was shameful.

Because pretending there had never been a break would be another kind of damage.

He touched a scar near his wrist.

It had faded enough that he sometimes forgot which hand it was on.

The body had kept the minutes better than he had.

Outside, the wind rose.

It did not arrive as metaphor.

It knocked over an empty plastic pot on the porch.

The pot rolled badly, stopped against the railing, shivered there.

He laughed.

For several minutes nothing profound happened.

This also seemed important.

He went walking.

At the edge of a field, goldenrod had gone brown.

Young trees were moving into land that had once been open.

He knew enough ecology now to recognize that a place could make itself hospitable to what would replace it.

One life altered the ground.

Another arrived because the ground had been altered.

There was no committee.

No blueprint of the forest hidden beneath the weeds.

The first plants did not fail when the trees came.

They had changed the conditions under which a tree could become possible.

He stood with his hands in his pockets and felt something in him loosen.

For years he had wanted to make the thing that lasted.

What if that was vanity?

What if the deepest work was sometimes to make possible the thing that made you unnecessary?

A crow called from the power line.

He looked up.

“Don't get carried away,” he said.

The crow left.

At home there were children's blocks scattered across a floor.

A plank was not yet a bridge.

That was its gift.

It might become a bridge.

Or a wall.

Or the edge of a kingdom.

Or nothing at all.

He picked it up and turned it over.

No inscription.

No instructions.

A thing could be generous by refusing to decide what it was for.

He put it back exactly where he had found it.

This small restraint pleased him more than it should have.

By evening the house was dark except for the kitchen.

On the table:

a book about singers who could make an ancient song new without making it cease to be the old song,

an article about a mathematical function that could tell when a single bit had changed,

a picture of a rebuilt shrine,

a photograph,

three pages of notes,

a coffee cup with a crack running from the rim halfway toward the handle.

He had repaired the cup once.

The repair was ugly.

He loved it more afterward.

He wondered whether this was wisdom or sentimentality.

He did not decide.

Rain began.

It touched everything without asking what anything meant.

Roof.

Asphalt.

Dead leaves.

The metal lid of the trash can.

A bird somewhere under cover.

The stones along the foundation.

He opened the window.

Cold air came into the room and moved across the loose pages.

One lifted.

Another.

The photograph slid beneath the book.

A page turned by itself and stopped.

For a moment he had the ridiculous feeling that the world was reading over his shoulder.

Then the rain blew in and wet the sill.

He closed the window.

There were things a man could change simply by touching them.

There were things he could only carry.

Things he could repair but should not disguise.

Things he could interpret forever and never revise.

Things whose disappearance completed them.

Things whose survival depended on being remade.

Things that changed him and vanished.

Things he would never know he had missed.

And perhaps there were things whose greatest gift was that, after passing through them, he could do something he had not been capable of doing before.

He looked again at the photograph.

For once he did not tell the boy what was coming.

He let him remain there.

Uncorrected.

Outside, the storm moved east.

The trees continued after he stopped listening.
