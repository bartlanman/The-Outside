# Chapter Two

# What Does the Joke Keep?

**διά ⇄ σύν**

---

The first time he understood the importance of a joke, someone had told one at a funeral.

It wasn't a good joke.

That may have been why it worked.

The dead man had owned a green lawn chair that he carried everywhere and refused to replace, although one arm had been repaired with silver tape and one of the aluminum legs had begun to buckle inward.

Someone said they should have buried him in it.

There was a moment when nobody knew what kind of room they were in anymore.

Then his widow laughed.

Not politely.

She bent forward and made a sound so sudden and animal that three other people started laughing because she was laughing, and then someone tried to stop and that made it worse.

For perhaps twenty seconds the dead man returned more completely than he had during any of the speeches.

Not because death had become less serious.

Because seriousness had become large enough to contain the ridiculous chair.

Years later he would remember this when people spoke about reverence.

He had once believed reverence meant leaving a thing untouched.

This turned out not to be true of anything he loved.

Children knew this before adults did.

Give a child a solemn sentence and sooner or later she would repeat it in the voice of a duck.

Tell her the correct way to march and she would march backward.

Give her a name long enough and someone would shorten it, stretch it, rhyme it with something disgusting, attach it to a story that never happened.

Some names survived this.

Others did not.

He began to wonder whether affection had always contained a small amount of vandalism.

The thought pleased him.

So naturally he distrusted it.

He had become suspicious of any idea that enjoyed being thought.

One evening he watched two improvisers walk onto an empty stage.

One said there was a horse in the room.

There was no horse.

The second did not inspect the claim.

He fed it.

Within minutes the horse had a name, an illness, a disputed inheritance and an unreasonable fear of ceiling fans.

Nobody had planned any of this.

The strangest part was not that they had invented a horse.

It was that each performer had surrendered the right to say what the horse was.

Whatever one person placed into the space became something the other had to encounter.

Freedom had entered through an obligation.

He wrote that down.

Then, remembering himself, he drew a line through it.

Across the internet a photograph of a man's face was being used for something the man had never meant.

He did not know the man.

He did not know the original circumstance.

The face had escaped.

Someone put words beneath it.

Someone changed the words.

Someone used the changed version as the basis for another version.

The man's expression became disappointment, suspicion, lust, exhaustion, capitalism, Tuesday.

After enough repetitions there were people who knew the face without knowing the man.

This bothered him.

It also fascinated him.

Something could become common by being repeatedly taken away from its beginning.

Each use damaged the original relation.

Each use strengthened another relation.

He could not decide whether this was survival.

He left the question open.

Once, reading about old festivals, he encountered a world in which kings could be mocked, solemnity dragged into the street, bodies exaggerated, hierarchies turned over for a day.

The priest met the drunk.

The crown met the ass.

The lofty found itself beside the obscene.

The order was not abolished.

Morning would come.

The king would still be king.

Perhaps this was precisely why the laughter mattered.

For a little while the world was forced to stand beside its own opposite.

He thought about all the people he knew who could endure criticism but not ridicule.

Ridicule was different.

Argument approached the gate carrying identification.

Laughter climbed the wall.

You could defend a proposition.

It was much harder to defend the sound you made when somebody imitated you perfectly.

He knew this because his daughter could do him.

Not his voice exactly.

His manner of beginning an explanation.

She would narrow her eyes, lift one finger and say:

“Okay, but here's the thing.”

The first time she did it he protested.

“I don't do that.”

This produced such an immediate reaction from everyone at the table that he knew he did.

He hated the impression for approximately four seconds.

Then he wanted her to do it again.

It was strange to be returned to oneself as a joke.

A mirror showed the face.

A mimic showed the habit.

Sometimes distortion was more accurate than accuracy.

That seemed dangerous enough to keep.

He began noticing that the things people mocked most successfully were rarely things they had failed to understand.

Bad parody merely announced dislike.

Good parody had studied the victim.

The sentence length.

The gesture.

The favorite escape.

The place where confidence became performance.

To make something ridiculous you sometimes had to know it intimately.

Hatred could do this.

So could love.

He had seen families reduce entire biographies to one phrase spoken in an absent person's voice.

He had seen friends insult one another with a precision strangers were not permitted.

He had seen an old photograph passed around a table until dignity had been completely destroyed and affection had somehow increased.

Perhaps intimacy was partly the right to deform without losing recognition.

Perhaps.

He could feel another clean sentence approaching and refused it entry.

Outside, somebody's dog barked at a leaf blower.

The leaf blower stopped.

The dog continued.

This was funnier than it should have been.

He stood at the window until the man started the machine again.

The dog erupted.

Machine off.

Silence.

Machine on.

Dog.

By the fourth repetition the man with the leaf blower was laughing.

The dog was not.

There was something pure in this arrangement.

Neither participant understood that a comedy had begun.

That night he dreamed that every law had been repealed.

At first everyone celebrated.

People drove wherever they wanted.

Restaurants served breakfast at midnight.

Children renamed the months.

A courthouse became a roller rink.

Then gradually the dream became unbearable.

Nobody could cheat because there were no rules.

Nobody could trespass because there were no boundaries.

Nobody could arrive late because nothing had a time.

Nobody could tell a joke because nothing was expected.

He woke before dawn with the peculiar relief of discovering the ceiling where he had left it.

Dark.

Flat.

Unamused.

He lay there thinking about constraint.

The joke did not destroy the rule.

The joke needed the rule.

The fool needed the crown.

The parody carried the original inside its distortion.

The improviser became free by accepting what had already entered the scene.

The child's mispronunciation required a word to mispronounce.

Even obscenity required something somewhere to have been declared clean.

He could feel the old temptation to arrange these observations into machinery.

He got out of bed instead.

In the kitchen he found a note taped to the refrigerator.

BUY COFFEE  
MILK  
DOG FOOD  
STOP EXPLAINING THINGS

He smiled.

The last line was in his daughter's handwriting.

He added:

NO.

Then beneath it:

BUT I'LL TRY.

In the morning someone had crossed out **TRY** and written:

**START NOW.**

There was no signature.

He left the note on the refrigerator.

For the next several months nobody removed it.

The paper curled at the corners.

A grease spot appeared beside COFFEE.

Someone drew a small crown over STOP.

Someone else gave the crown legs.

The legs acquired shoes.

The shoes became a man.

The man was eventually given a speech bubble.

Nobody remembered who had begun it.

The grocery list stopped being useful.

It became something else.

One afternoon he reached for it.

His hand stopped before the paper.

He realized that taking it down now would feel like destroying something.

This amused him.

A thing nobody had intended to make had somehow acquired the right to remain.

He closed the refrigerator.

The little crowned man said nothing.

For once neither did he.
