# Chapter Three

# How Far Apart Is Together?

**διά ⇄ σύν**

---

There was a distance at which his wife became beautiful again.

Not the distance of leaving.

Nothing so dramatic.

Twenty feet, perhaps.

Across a grocery store.

Across a parking lot.

Across a room when she did not know he was looking.

Up close there were instructions.

Did you call him back?

We need detergent.

The light in the hall is still out.

Your mother called.

Up close there were pores and interrupted sentences and the small administrative weather of having spent years beside another person.

But sometimes he would see her from the far end of an aisle standing beneath the fluorescent lights with a jar in one hand, reading the label.

And for several seconds she belonged to the world again.

Not to him.

The feeling was almost alarming.

He had not lost her.

He had merely stopped being the only distance from which she could be seen.

He began noticing distance after that.

Not absence.

Distance.

They were different things.

A door left open between two rooms.

The pause after someone said your name.

The inch of air between two hands before one decided whether to take the other.

He had always thought the important part was the contact.

Perhaps because contact announced itself.

A hand touched a shoulder.

A cup struck a table.

A mouth met another mouth.

Contact made witnesses of the senses.

Distance was harder.

Distance could be mistaken for nothing.

One afternoon he watched two dancers.

They moved close enough that he could not always tell who had initiated a turn.

A shoulder accepted weight.

A back became a surface.

A hand found a forearm and then lost it.

At one point the woman leaned so far from her own balance that, for a second, her body belonged partly to the man's willingness to remain where he was.

Then he moved.

She fell through the place where he had been.

Not far.

Enough.

The audience inhaled.

She caught herself on one palm, rolled, and stood.

The man did not apologize.

The dance continued.

He thought about trust.

Then thought better of it.

Trust was too clean a word for what he had seen.

There had been gravity.

There had been weight.

There had been the possibility of being dropped.

There had been two bodies that could only move together because neither stopped being separate.

He wrote:

TOUCH REQUIRES—

and stopped.

The page waited.

He disliked when paper looked expectant.

He closed the notebook.

Later, driving home, he pulled over to watch birds.

Hundreds of them.

They moved above a field in a black shape that refused to remain one shape long enough to be named.

It stretched.

Folded.

Narrowed.

Turned.

Opened again.

He searched for the bird in charge.

This embarrassed him almost immediately.

There was no front for long enough.

No commander visible.

No bird carrying a diagram.

Still, the flock turned.

He wondered where the turn existed before it happened.

In one bird?

Between two?

In a pressure no bird could see?

The flock passed over him and became briefly so thin he could see the evening through it.

Then it thickened again.

He thought:

There is no place where the whole is.

This frightened him a little.

He liked wholes to have addresses.

A house.

A book.

A body.

A name.

Something you could point at and say:

There.

That is the thing.

But perhaps some things were only there when enough separate things remained in relation.

He watched until the flock was too far away to distinguish from weather.

At home he played a recording of two choirs singing back and forth across a church.

One voice rose.

Then stopped.

Another answered from somewhere he could not see.

The silence between them became unbearable once he noticed it.

Not empty.

Waiting.

The first group could not answer while it was still singing.

Someone had to stop.

He replayed the passage.

Voice.

Silence.

Answer.

Again.

Voice.

Silence.

Answer.

The music did not cross the church despite the empty space.

It crossed because of it.

He stood in the kitchen listening.

The refrigerator clicked off.

For a few seconds the house was quieter than he had realized it could be.

He heard a car pass outside.

Water moving through a pipe.

Someone upstairs opening a drawer.

He wondered how many things had been occurring all day beneath the things he had been doing.

On the table was a piece of lace his grandmother had kept in a drawer.

He did not know whether she had made it.

He wished now that he had asked.

The older he became, the more objects turned into unanswered questions.

He held it against the window.

Thread.

Hole.

Thread.

Hole.

He tried looking only at the thread.

Then only at the openings.

He could not keep either one separate for long.

The pattern required both.

Cut away enough cloth and somehow the absence stopped being damage.

It became design.

He put the lace down carefully.

There were limits to this, obviously.

A hole in a roof was not architecture simply because light entered through it.

A missing tooth was not lace.

A person who did not return had not created a meaningful interval.

He knew enough by then to distrust any idea capable of making loss elegant.

There were absences that produced nothing.

Rooms that remained empty.

Calls that did not come.

Names nobody answered to anymore.

A gap could hold a song.

A gap could also be where the song ended.

This seemed important.

He left it unresolved.

Several weeks later he went to a museum and stood in front of a cast of the space beneath a piece of furniture.

At first he did not understand what he was looking at.

The object appeared heavy.

Solid.

Obstinate.

Then he realized the sculpture was not the furniture.

It was where the furniture had not been.

The space under.

The volume around.

The air ordinarily dismissed as nothing had been given weight.

He walked around it twice.

People leaned toward the wall label.

He did not.

For once he preferred being slightly wrong.

It seemed astonishing that absence had required so much material to become visible.

He thought of a chair after someone stood up.

A bed after a body left it.

The indentation in a pillow.

The two inches between people during an argument.

The six inches afterward.

The hallway after a door closed.

The particular silence after a question for which both people knew the answer.

He became uncomfortable.

Art did that occasionally.

Most of the time museums only made his feet hurt.

He left.

Outside, two strangers were trying to carry a large table through a doorway.

“Your side.”

“My side?”

“Turn.”

“I am turning.”

“No, the other—”

They stopped.

Backed up.

Laughed.

Tried again.

For several minutes the table belonged neither inside nor outside.

It existed diagonally between intentions.

Eventually one person lowered his end while the other lifted, and the table passed.

Neither one could have done it alone.

Neither one had known the exact movement before the doorway required it.

The doorway had participated.

This pleased him enormously.

He nearly wrote it down.

Instead he kept walking.

By then he was beginning to understand that not every thought improved by being captured.

Some thoughts behaved better when allowed to follow for a while.

That evening his daughter called.

They spoke about nothing consequential.

A teacher she disliked.

A friend who had become strange.

Something she had ordered that had arrived broken.

Then there was a silence.

He almost filled it.

He was good at filling silence.

Advice.

Questions.

Stories from his own life disguised as advice.

He could feel several sentences lining up.

He let none of them out.

The silence lengthened.

He wondered if the call had dropped.

Then she said,

“Dad?”

“Yeah.”

Another pause.

“I don't know.”

He waited.

Neither did he.

For once this seemed like enough.

After the call he remained in the chair.

Nothing had been solved.

Nothing had even been properly stated.

Still, something had happened.

He could feel it.

Not in what either of them had said.

Somewhere else.

He thought about the dancers.

The birds.

The choirs.

The lace.

The cast air beneath the table.

The doorway.

His daughter on the other end of a line saying she didn't know.

He felt the old machinery starting.

Relation.

Negative space.

Emergence.

Distributed—

He laughed.

“Not tonight,” he said.

The room did not argue.

Wind moved lightly against the house.

A picture frame on the wall had been hanging crooked for three days.

He had noticed it every time he passed.

He stood to straighten it.

Then stopped.

The frame leaned perhaps half an inch to the left.

Between level and crooked there was such a small distance.

He looked at it.

Sat back down.

For no reason he could defend, he left it that way.

In the morning sunlight entered along one edge of the frame and made a thin bright line against the wall.

He had never seen it before.
