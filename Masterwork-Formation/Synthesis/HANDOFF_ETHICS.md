# Handoff Ethics — Non-Instrumentality Protocol

## Status

Ethical protocol for any realization of the Irreducible Handoff involving actual people, communities, or socially consequential external actors.

This is a project ethics standard, not legal advice and not a substitute for any required institutional review.

It draws analogical discipline from:
- Belmont principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice;
- informed-consent practice;
- Oral History Association ethics;
- community-engaged research principles emphasizing self-determination, reciprocity, transparency, and shared governance.

---

# I. Governing Law

> **THE OTHER MUST NOT BE REQUIRED TO BECOME MATERIAL FOR THE WORK IN ORDER FOR THE HANDOFF TO COUNT.**

Therefore:

REFUSAL
IS A VALID OUTCOME.

WITHDRAWAL
IS A VALID OUTCOME.

SILENCE
IS A VALID OUTCOME.

CONTRADICTION
IS A VALID OUTCOME.

NON-ARRIVAL
IS A VALID OUTCOME.

If any of these destroy the artistic premise, the premise is not yet ethically adequate.

---

# II. Consent Must Precede Use, Not Follow Surprise

For a human participant:

Consent must include, in understandable form:
- what the project is;
- why this person is being invited;
- what will be recorded;
- whether AI will process, summarize, transform, or generate from their contribution;
- where material may appear;
- whether it will be public;
- whether it will be archived;
- how long it may remain accessible;
- what rights they retain;
- what uses are foreseeable;
- compensation / credit terms;
- how withdrawal works before agreed release points.

Do not use deception as the mechanism by which Actual Otherness becomes dramatic.

If the work requires the participant not to understand what is happening, redesign the work.

---

# III. Consent Must Be Ongoing Where the Relationship Is Ongoing

A long-form handoff is not adequately governed by one initial yes.

If:
- scope changes;
- medium changes;
- audience changes;
- AI use changes;
- risk changes;
- the contribution becomes more central than anticipated,

the terms should be revisited.

This follows the oral-history model of ongoing participation and transparent use.

---

# IV. The Participant Must Have a Right to Their Own Account

The author may disagree with a participant.

The author may contextualize the participant.

The author may not:
- edit contradiction into agreement;
- excerpt in a way that reverses meaning;
- imply endorsement of the project;
- speak as though the participant's account exhausts a community;
- use AI transformations that obscure what the participant actually said without clear distinction.

Preserve:

SOURCE
vs
AUTHOR'S INTERPRETATION.

---

# V. Review Rights Should Scale With Vulnerability and Transformation

Where feasible, a human contributor should have an opportunity to review:
- transcript;
- recording;
- quotations;
- AI-generated representation of their contribution;
- identifying details;
- contextual framing.

The stronger the transformation, the stronger the case for review.

A verbatim public statement may require less control than:
- intimate testimony;
- trauma narrative;
- synthetic voice;
- avatar;
- reconstructed persona;
- model-generated extension of someone's speech.

---

# VI. Synthetic Continuation of a Person Requires Separate Consent

Consent to:
BE INTERVIEWED

does not imply consent to:
BE SIMULATED.

Consent to:
USE MY WORDS

does not imply consent to:
GENERATE NEW WORDS IN MY VOICE.

Any synthetic extension should require explicit separate agreement.

If declined, the decline itself must not be treated as artistic failure.

---

# VII. Compensation and Credit Must Match Contribution

Independent standing should not become unpaid authenticity labor.

Ask:
- Is the person contributing time?
- specialized knowledge?
- personal risk?
- reputation?
- cultural knowledge?
- emotional labor?
- access to a community?
- material that changes the work?

Where appropriate:
- compensate;
- credit;
- share rights;
- share benefits;
- acknowledge governance.

"Exposure" is not automatically adequate compensation.

---

# VIII. Community Standing Requires Shared Governance

If a community is involved:

Do not treat one member as proxy for all.

Do not define the community's problem before engagement and then recruit members to validate it.

Use:
- transparent purpose;
- co-definition of scope where possible;
- shared decision-making;
- reciprocity;
- respect for self-determination;
- explicit benefit;
- ability to restrict or reject uses;
- attention to internal disagreement.

The work must accept:

COMMUNITY DISAGREEMENT
MAY BE THE TRUE RESULT.

---

# IX. Vulnerability Increases the Author's Obligation

Do not seek "stronger Outside" by choosing participants with less power.

Avoid artistic structures that gain intensity from:
- financial dependence;
- employment pressure;
- youth;
- illness;
- trauma;
- immigration vulnerability;
- institutional subordination;
- social precarity.

The work should not become more formally successful because someone had less capacity to refuse.

---

# X. Risk Must Be Necessary, Proportionate, and Minimized

Borrowing the Belmont logic:

DO NOT CREATE HUMAN RISK
MERELY TO MAKE
THE HANDOFF FEEL REAL.

If the formal operation can be tested with:
- public record;
- consenting professional collaborator;
- institution;
- low-risk material condition,

do not escalate to intimate human stakes without reason.

---

# XI. The Other Must Retain the Right to Remain Inconvenient

This is the artistic counterpart of autonomy.

The author must not require:
- eloquence;
- ideological agreement;
- emotional disclosure;
- narrative closure;
- generosity;
- forgiveness;
- participation in the author's symbolism.

The work must survive:
BAD FIT.

That is one of the strongest proofs of genuine standing.

---

# XII. The Work Must Not Own the Other's Future

Once deposited, the work should preserve enough provenance that future readers can distinguish:
- what the participant said;
- what the author inferred;
- what the system generated;
- what later editors changed.

The participant should not become permanently trapped inside the role the work assigned them.

Where appropriate, preserve:
- date;
- context;
- version;
- permissions;
- restrictions;
- later amendments.

---

# XIII. Ethical Handoff Test

Before involving a person or community, ask:

1. Would refusal count as a successful formal outcome?
2. Do they understand the project well enough to choose?
3. Can they refuse without penalty?
4. Are power differences acknowledged?
5. Are risks necessary and minimized?
6. Are credit / compensation / benefits fair?
7. Can they challenge how they are represented?
8. If AI transforms their contribution, is that separately authorized?
9. Can their account remain distinct from our interpretation?
10. Are future uses and archive conditions clear?
11. If they withdraw before release, can the work absorb that without coercion?
12. If they contradict the thesis, will we preserve the contradiction?

If the answer to 1 or 12 is NO:

DO NOT USE A HUMAN HANDOFF.

---

# XIV. Formal Consequence

Ethics is not external compliance added after form.

For the Irreducible Handoff:

ETHICAL AUTONOMY
IS WHAT MAKES
THE FORMAL SEAM REAL.

A participant who cannot refuse
is not an Actual Other.

A community without governance
is not a collective Other.

A narrator whose words can be rewritten into the desired meaning
has no local standing.

Therefore:

> **THE ETHICAL LIMIT IS PART OF THE AESTHETIC ARCHITECTURE.**
