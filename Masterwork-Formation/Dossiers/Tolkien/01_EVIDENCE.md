# Tolkien — Evidence Notes

## Archive condition

Status:

RICH.

Marquette University's Tolkien collection contains:
- original manuscripts
- multiple working drafts
- plot notes
- maps
- chronologies
- rejected passages
- proofs
- linguistic notes
- several drafts of a rejected epilogue

For *The Lord of the Rings* alone, Marquette records more than 7,000 leaves / more than 9,000 pages of manuscript material.

Some chapters survive in very numerous draft states.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Composition chronology

Tolkien began a sequel to *The Hobbit* in December 1937.

The composition of *The Lord of the Rings* extended through 1949, with publication in three volumes in 1954–55.

The drafting process was highly non-linear:
- rewriting earlier chapters,
- changing names and identities,
- revising plot structures,
- drawing maps,
- constructing chronologies,
- integrating the older legendarium.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## The sequel was not originally conceived as the final published work

Tolkien's December 1937 letter to Stanley Unwin shows uncertainty about what a sequel to *The Hobbit* could be.

He was already preoccupied with his older mythology and invented languages.

The new work gradually became entangled with that larger legendarium.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Languages precede and generate world

Tolkien's invented languages predate much of the narrative world.

He explicitly said that the languages were foundational and that stories were developed partly to provide a world in which those languages could exist historically.

Modern linguistic scholarship on Tolkien confirms the long co-development of:
- phonology,
- grammar,
- invented linguistic history,
- peoples,
- geography,
- mythology.

Status:
DOCUMENTED / PRIMARY AUTHORIAL EVIDENCE + STRONG SCHOLARLY SUPPORT.

## Maps as compositional instrument

Tolkien drew and revised maps while composing.

The Tolkien Estate notes that maps were integral to world-building and that the *Lord of the Rings* map physically grew as the story expanded.

Some maps were drawn to plot precise distances and action in later books.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Secondary World / sub-creation

"On Fairy-Stories" gives Tolkien's explicit theory of sub-creation.

The maker creates a Secondary World whose internal relations should support the reader's secondary belief.

Tolkien also emphasizes Recovery, Escape, and Consolation as functions of fairy-story.

Status:
PRIMARY TEXT / AUTHORIAL THEORY.

## World not reducible to allegory

Tolkien repeatedly resisted simple one-to-one topical allegory.

In correspondence he allowed that stories can acquire broad applicability and symbolic force while rejecting reductive claims that particular plot elements directly encode specific contemporary events.

Status:
DOCUMENTED AUTHORIAL POSITION.

Caution:
This does not mean history is irrelevant to the work.

## Deep time

*The Lord of the Rings* is full of:
- ruins
- songs
- older languages
- genealogies
- remembered wars
- vanished kingdoms
- surviving beings from earlier ages
- references to lands no longer present on the current map

Christopher Tolkien emphasizes that the Third Age story is embedded within the much older First and Second Age legendarium.

Status:
TEXTUALLY DOCUMENTED / AUTHORIAL ARCHIVE SUPPORT.

## World War II

Tolkien composed much of *The Lord of the Rings* during World War II.

He served as an Air Raid Warden in Oxford; his sons served or trained in the armed forces.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

Caution:
Do not reduce the Ring to the atomic bomb or the War of the Ring to World War II.

## "The Scouring of the Shire"

The chapter survives in multiple manuscript states.

Marquette records:
- an earlier rejected title, "The Mending of the Shire"
- multiple holograph drafts
- notes
- printer typescript

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

Research implication:

The return-home sequence was not incidental late decoration; it received sustained compositional work.

## Rejected epilogue

Tolkien drafted an epilogue set after the main ending, focused in part on Sam and his children.

Multiple epilogue drafts survive, but Tolkien ultimately omitted the section.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

Research implication:

Tolkien deliberately stopped before one further stage of narrated domestic succession.

## Return and altered home

The hobbits return after the defeat of Sauron to find the Shire politically and physically changed.

They must act using capacities acquired during the larger journey.

Status:
TEXTUALLY DOCUMENTED.

## Frodo's non-restoration

Frodo returns home but cannot simply resume his prior life.

His wounds, memory, and relation to the Ring remain consequential.

He eventually departs over Sea.

Status:
TEXTUALLY DOCUMENTED.

## Sam and stewardship

Sam returns, helps restore the Shire, marries, raises a family, becomes Mayor, and participates in long-term local renewal.

Status:
TEXTUALLY DOCUMENTED.

Research implication:

The end distinguishes at least two kinds of return:
- return that cannot restore the traveler,
- return that becomes stewardship.

## World continues after central quest

Appendices, genealogies, chronologies, later ages, surviving characters, and the wider legendarium all reinforce that destruction of the Ring does not end the world.

Status:
TEXTUALLY DOCUMENTED.

## Possessive sub-creation

In his 1951 letter to Milton Waldman, Tolkien explicitly warns that the sub-creator can fall into possessiveness, treating private creation as something to dominate and becoming a would-be lord over it.

Status:
PRIMARY TEXT.

Research implication:

Tolkien's own sub-creation theory contains an internal warning against sovereign world ownership.

## Major caution

Do not state:
- that Middle-earth literally has independent existence from Tolkien;
- that Tolkien's world is "autonomous" in a metaphysical sense;
- that all world-building is Tolkienian;
- that deep lore by itself creates independent standing;
- that the Shire episode is merely an allegory of postwar England;
- that Frodo's wounds have one simple biographical source;
- that the world was fully designed before the plot.

The archive shows extensive emergence, revision, and retroactive integration.
