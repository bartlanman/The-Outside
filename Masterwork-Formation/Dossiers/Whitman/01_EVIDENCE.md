## Lifelong revision

Whitman published six distinct American editions of *Leaves of Grass* during his lifetime:
- 1855
- 1856
- 1860–61
- 1867
- 1871–72
- 1881–82

The 1891–92 so-called "deathbed edition" is primarily a reprinting of the 1881 edition with annexes and smaller revisions rather than a wholly new edition.

By Whitman's death, *Leaves* had grown from a 95-page volume of twelve untitled poems to a work of more than 400 pages.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Bookmaking

Whitman self-published the 1855 edition.

He:
- designed the binding,
- chose type,
- designed pages,
- worked with the engraver on the frontispiece,
- supervised printing,
- and reportedly set some type himself.

His printing background remained important throughout his life, and he continued to supervise material features of later editions closely.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Material constraint

The 1855 book's form was partly conditioned by:
- limited money,
- available paper,
- the Rome printing shop,
- press and type constraints,
- the need to fit poems into a feasible number of signatures.

Whitman adjusted arrangement and page use while the book was being produced.

Status:
DOCUMENTED / STRONG BOOK-HISTORY RECONSTRUCTION.

## Early formation

Whitman's notebooks contain trial lines related to the 1855 poems.

The Library of Congress notes that an early notebook bearing an 1847 internal date contains trial lines for material that would become part of "Song of Myself," probably entered closer to 1854.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Self-fashioning and publication

The 1855 edition did not put Whitman's name on the title page.

Instead it placed a full-body working-man portrait opposite the title page.

Whitman also anonymously wrote or placed several favorable self-reviews of the 1855 *Leaves*.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

Research implication:
The "self" in *Leaves* is not simply private autobiography. Whitman actively constructed a public poetic persona and its reception.

## Emerson

Ralph Waldo Emerson responded enthusiastically to the 1855 edition, welcoming Whitman at the start of what he predicted would be a major career.

Whitman publicized Emerson's approval in ways that later strained their relationship.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## 1860 expansion

The 1860–61 edition added 146 poems to the prior 32 and introduced major thematic clusters.

Important clusters include:
- "Calamus"
- "Enfans d'Adam" / "Children of Adam"
- democratic and national groupings

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## "Calamus"

The "Calamus" cluster grew in part from the "Live Oak, with Moss" sequence.

Scholarship commonly reads the poems as combining intimate male-male affection with Whitman's democratic theory of comradeship.

Status:
TEXTUALLY DOCUMENTED / BIOGRAPHICAL INTERPRETATION REQUIRES CAUTION.

## Civil War encounter

Whitman traveled south in 1862 after learning that his brother George had been wounded.

He remained in Washington and spent extensive time visiting wounded soldiers in military hospitals.

His hospital notebooks record:
- names,
- addresses,
- wounds,
- needs,
- requested food or writing supplies,
- messages to families,
- direct observations of suffering and death.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## War changes poetic practice

Whitman Archive scholarship argues that wartime encounters generated a new kind of poem:
- less dominated by the expansive self,
- more attentive to particular bodies and suffering,
- more aware of death as irreducibly horrifying.

*Drum-Taps* was initially conceived as a separate project.

Whitman later struggled for years to incorporate the Civil War poems into *Leaves of Grass*.

Status:
DOCUMENTED FORMATION HISTORY / SCHOLARLY INTERPRETATION OF VOICE CHANGE.

## Iterative postwar reconstruction

The 1867 edition physically combined separately paginated parts and has been described as a "workshop" edition.

Whitman continued rearranging and redistributing war poems through later editions.

The 1871–72 edition dispersed the Civil War material through multiple clusters, making the war alter the structure of the larger work.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Racial contradiction

Whitman's poetic work can enact radically egalitarian identification with Black figures, including the fugitive slave and enslaved people at auction.

His journalism, private comments, and later political thought also contain racist assumptions, white supremacist attitudes, and opposition to racial equality inconsistent with the most egalitarian moments of his poetry.

His treatment of Native Americans similarly combines admiration, linguistic and cultural interest, and poetic inclusion with settler-colonial assumptions and "vanishing Indian" ideology.

Status:
DOCUMENTED / EXTENSIVELY STUDIED.

Research implication:

THE EXPANSIVE "I" CANNOT BE TREATED AS AUTOMATICALLY DEMOCRATIC MERELY BECAUSE IT CLAIMS INCLUSION.

## Reception

The 1855 *Leaves* was startling to many reviewers because of:
- free verse,
- bodily candor,
- sexual material,
- unusual typography,
- unconventional persona.

Reception ranged from enthusiastic recognition to charges of vulgarity, obscenity, egotism, and formlessness.

Status:
DOCUMENTED.

## Major caution

Do not collapse:
WHITMAN THE HISTORICAL PERSON
into
THE "I" OF THE POEMS.

But do not detach them absolutely either.

Whitman deliberately blurred:
- body and book,
- poet and persona,
- self and nation,
- private life and public address.

The dossier must track the operation without treating poetic identification as transparent biography.
